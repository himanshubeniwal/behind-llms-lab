The HPC lab

Seven small exercises. Each one is a folder with a short Python script and the Slurm job script that runs it. You start with a job that says hello and end with a real language model answering your prompts on a GPU.

How every exercise works#

Go into the folder, make a folder for the logs, send the job, and read the log when it is done. That is the whole rhythm of working on a cluster.

Each exercise starts with a line about Marie and Martin, two students who made the typical mistake there before you. You need the environment from Setup for exercises 3 to 6. The first two only use the Python that is already on the cluster. Our project is p_lv_llmsecrets, and it is already written into every job script.

Your first batch job#

01_helloany clusterCPU onlytime limit 5 min

Marie and Martin. Martin submits without a logs folder. The job is gone a second later and there is nothing to read. See what happened

You practise: your first sbatch, the variables Slurm gives a job, and why --cpus-per-task matters.

The Python script prints where it runs and what Slurm gave it. Then it estimates π by throwing random darts at a square, split over all the CPU cores the job got.

What to look for in the log

  • Hello from shows the name of a compute node, not of the login node you typed on.
  • os.cpu_count() is every core of that node. CPUs I may use is 4, because the job script asked for 4. Slurm keeps you inside what you asked for.
  • The lines starting with SLURM_ are environment variables that every job gets: its ID, its name, the project, the node.
  • The last two lines give π and the speed in million samples per second.

Try it. Options on the command line win over the #SBATCH lines, so you can change the number of cores without editing the file. Compare the speed.

Reading the job script

LineWhat it says

Watch a job, then cancel it#

02_cancelany clusterCPU onlytime limit 10 min

Marie and Martin. Marie sees PD, decides the job is broken and submits it three times. Martin tries to stop a job with Ctrl+C. See what happened

You practise: squeue, scontrol, scancel, sacct, and saving your work when the job is stopped.

This job is slow on purpose. It prints one line every 10 seconds for 8 minutes, so you have time to look at it from the outside and then stop it.

What to look for

When you cancel a job, Slurm first sends the signal SIGTERM, which means “please stop”, and only later SIGKILL. The script catches SIGTERM and writes a small checkpoint file. A real training script should do the same. The end of the log looks like this:

Bonus: a job that waits on purpose. The REASON column of squeue tells you why a job is not running yet. whypending is a ZIH helper that explains it in more words.

Ask for a GPU and measure it#

03_gpuAlpha or Capella1 GPUtime limit 10 min

Marie and Martin. Martin deletes the --gres line because it looks like a comment, and the GPU is gone. See what happened

You practise: --gres=gpu:1, nvidia-smi, and the difference between a CPU and a GPU.

The script multiplies two big matrices, which is the main work inside every layer of a language model. It does it first on the CPU cores and then on the GPU, and prints the speed in TFLOP/s, that is 1012 floating point operations per second.

What to look for in the log

  • The table from nvidia-smi shows exactly one GPU, an A100 on Alpha or an H100 on Capella. The node has more, but you only see the one you asked for.
  • CUDA available: True, and the name and memory of the GPU.
  • Three speed lines: the CPU with 32-bit numbers, the GPU with 32-bit numbers, and the GPU with 16-bit numbers (bfloat16). How many times faster is the GPU? What does it gain from the smaller numbers?

The line that gets you a GPU is #SBATCH --gres=gpu:1. Without it the job still runs on a GPU cluster, but the script prints No GPU found. And every cluster has its own Slurm, so a job sent from Barnard never gets a GPU.

Try it. Everything in the last line of the job script can be changed. Make the matrices smaller or bigger with --n and see when the GPU stops being worth it.

Train a tiny GPT#

04_tinygptAlpha or Capella1 GPUtime limit 30 min

Marie and Martin. Martin asks for 6 attention heads and the job fails within seconds. Marie finds the reason in the .err file. See what happened

You practise: a real training run as a batch job, watching the loss in the log, and using the result afterwards.

The model is a small GPT that reads and writes single characters. It is the same model you build step by step in notebook 5: 4 Transformer blocks, about 0.8 million parameters. It trains for 5000 steps on tiny Shakespeare, about 1 MB of plays in one text file.

What to look for in the log

  • The first line shows device=cuda, the size of the vocabulary (65 characters) and the number of parameters.
  • Every 250 steps there is a line with the training loss, the validation loss and the speed in tokens per second.
  • A model that knows nothing has a loss of log 65 ≈ 4.17. The counting model from notebook 4 gets stuck around 2.5. Where does this one end up?
  • At the end the model writes 500 characters of its own Shakespeare.

The job writes three files into $WS_DIR/runs/tinygpt-<jobid>: ckpt.pt with the weights, metrics.json with the final losses, and samples.txt with the generated text.

Let the model write

Try it: a bigger model. Everything after the name of the job script is passed on to Python.

Try it: a job that runs out of time. Is ckpt.pt still saved? Look at the line --signal=TERM@60 in the job script and at on_sigterm in the Python script.

A learning-rate sweep with a job array#

05_sweepAlpha or Capella4 × 1 GPUtime limit 20 min each

Marie and Martin. Marie starts the sweep from a fresh terminal and the script refuses to run. See what happened

You practise: job arrays with --array, and jobs that wait for other jobs with --dependency=afterok.

Which learning rate is best for the tiny GPT? Instead of trying four values one after the other, you send one array job. Slurm starts the same script four times, each on its own GPU. The only thing that changes is $SLURM_ARRAY_TASK_ID, which goes from 0 to 3 and picks the learning rate. A second, tiny job waits until all four are done and prints a table.

What to look for

  • squeue shows four tasks of one array, and the collect job waiting with the reason Dependency.
  • Each task writes its own log, logs/lr-sweep-<jobid>_0.out to _3.out.
  • When all are done, logs/lr-collect-<jobid>.out holds the result, best run first:

Try it. Cancel just one task of a running array with scancel <jobid>_3. What happens to the collect job now? Then change --array=0-3 to --array=0-3%2, so that at most two tasks run at the same time. Or add more learning rates to the list LEARNING_RATES and make the array longer.

Run a pretrained LLM#

06_hf_generateAlpha or Capella1 GPUtime limit 15 min

Marie and Martin. Martin skips the download, and the job fails. Then the model finally answers the question about Dresden. See what happened

You practise: getting a model from Hugging Face into your workspace, and running it over a whole file of prompts as a GPU job.

The model is Qwen/Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct. It has 0.5 billion parameters, speaks many languages and needs about 1 GB of GPU memory. First you download it once on the login node. Then the job loads it from your workspace and answers every line of prompts.txt.

What to look for in the log

  • HF_HOME points into your workspace, not into your home folder.
  • The model line shows the number of parameters, cuda and torch.bfloat16.
  • Every prompt is printed with its answer. The answers are also saved as outputs-<jobid>.jsonl, one JSON object per line.
  • The last line gives the speed in new tokens per second. Compare it with the same model in notebook 6 on Colab.

Try it. Do the German and Hindi prompts get answers in the same language? Add your own lines to prompts.txt. Change --temperature or --max-new-tokens. Or try another model. The job runs offline, so download the model first:

GPT-2 only knows English and has no chat format, so it simply continues your text. For CPU against GPU, copy the job script, remove the --gres line and ask for more CPU cores.
