Step 1 · the servers

Meet the machines

Training a language model is mostly multiplying very large matrices, very many times. A laptop can do that for a toy model. For everything else there is a room full of computers in Dresden, and you are allowed to use it.

This page goes with the slides of the session: Introduction to HPC (Google Slides).

What a cluster is#

High performance computing, HPC for short, means using many strong computers together. The systems at TU Dresden are run by ZIH, the university's computing centre. They all run Linux, and you use them from a terminal.

The most important thing to understand on day one: a cluster is not one big computer that you log in to and then use. It is hundreds of separate computers, called nodes, that many people share. You never start your work on them by hand. You describe what you need, and a scheduler finds a free place and runs it for you.

Your computerA terminal. On the university network or the VPN.
→
Login nodeWhere ssh takes you. Edit files, use git, send jobs. No heavy work here.
→
SlurmThe scheduler. It keeps the queue and decides which job runs where and when.
→
Compute nodesWhere your job really runs, with the CPU cores, memory and GPUs you asked for.
Shared storage. Login nodes and compute nodes see the same files: your small home folder and the big workspaces. That is how a job finds your code and how you find its results.

The four parts in one sentence each

  • Login node. The front door. Everybody shares it, so it is for light work only: editing, git, looking at results, sending jobs. A training run on a login node slows down everyone and can get stopped.
  • Slurm. The batch system. You tell it how many cores, how much memory, how many GPUs and how much time you need. It puts your job in a queue and starts it when the resources are free.
  • Compute node. One computer of the cluster. You do not log in to it. Slurm starts your program there.
  • Job. One piece of work you hand to Slurm, usually as a small shell script with #SBATCH lines at the top. Exercise 1 is exactly that.

Why a language model needs this#

Inside every layer of a language model, the main work is a matrix multiplication. A GPU is a processor that does thousands of these small multiplications at the same time. In exercise 3 you measure the difference yourself: the same multiplication, once on CPU cores and once on a GPU.

The second reason is memory. A model has to fit into the memory of the GPU. The small chat model from exercise 6 has 0.5 billion parameters and needs about 1 GB. The models people use at work are many times bigger. The GPUs here have 40 GB and 94 GB each.

The third reason is that research means running things many times. A job array starts the same script with four learning rates on four GPUs at once (exercise 5). On a laptop you would wait four times as long.

Where your files live#

There are two kinds of storage, and mixing them up is the most common beginner problem.

Your home folder

$HOME is permanent and backed up, and it is limited to 50 GiB. It is for your code and your settings. If pip and Hugging Face put their caches and models there, it fills up and you get Disk quota exceeded. With a full home folder you cannot submit jobs any more.

A workspace

A big, fast folder that you ask for with one command. It has an expiry date, and you can extend it. This is where the Python environment, the data, the models and the results of this lab go.

You do not have to type this yourself. The setup script creates the workspace llm-lab for 30 days and moves all caches into it. horse is the name of a file system. There are several, and they differ in how long a workspace may live:

From the workspaces page of the Compendium, October 2026. The commands to list, extend and delete a workspace are on the cheat sheet.

A workspace ends. When the time is over the data is gone after the grace period. Copy what you want to keep, or extend the workspace in time with ws_extend. You can also ask for an e-mail reminder.

Three ways to work#

the normal way

A batch job

You write a job script and send it with sbatch. It runs when there is room, with or without you. The output goes to a log file. All exercises work like this.

for trying things

An interactive job

srun --pty bash -l gives you a terminal on a compute node. Good for installing, testing and debugging. The setup step uses it. Type exit to give the machine back.

for notebooks

JupyterHub

At jupyterhub.hpc.tu-dresden.de you start a notebook server on the cluster from the browser. The setup script adds a kernel called LLM Lab (venv-llm) for it.

What you need to get in#

  1. A ZIH login. The same user name and password you use for other university services.
  2. Membership in the course project. On the cluster, computing time belongs to projects. Ours is called p_lv_llmsecrets, and every job script names it with --account. You get the link to join from the instructors in the session. A new membership needs some time before it works on all clusters, so join first and read on while you wait.
  3. The university network or the VPN. The login nodes and JupyterHub cannot be reached from outside.

Then continue with Setup. More about access is in the Compendium.

Words you will hear#

WordWhat it means here

coreOne processor core. You ask for them with --cpus-per-task.
jobOne piece of work you hand to Slurm. It gets a number, the job ID.
job scriptA shell script whose first lines start with #SBATCH and say what the job needs.
project, accountThe group that the computing time is booked on. Ours is p_lv_llmsecrets.
partitionA group of nodes inside a cluster with its own limits.
walltimeThe time limit of a job, as on a clock on the wall. When it is over, Slurm stops the job.
moduleSoftware the cluster provides, switched on with module load.
workspaceA big folder with an expiry date.
